Weights move the line

Drop quality to zero and cheap fragile crossovers climb onto the frontier. That's the model telling the truth: they are cheap, and they will not survive 180,000 miles.

Each slider carries a provenance tag, because the six axes are not equally trustworthy. Cost is computed from the workbook. Efficiency is EPA and observed MPG. Comfort is the strongest of the subjective three — it starts from actual decibel-meter readings at 55 mph (median across measured trims), then subtracts points for documented seat complaints and body-on-frame ride harshness; models tagged "estimated" had no published reading. Longevity uses odds of reaching 250,000 miles against each study's own baseline, but 42 of 76 vehicles have no published figure. Quality and Reliability are SWAGs — quality from drivetrain architecture, reliability from Consumer Reports brand record minus documented mid-life failure patterns. Neither is measured data.

If you want to replace the two SWAGs with real open data: NHTSA publishes complaints, recalls, and TSBs as free keyless APIs and daily bulk files (public domain), and the UK DVSA publishes every MOT test result — including mileage at failure and coded failure reasons — under the Open Government Licence. That's the only free dataset anywhere that measures failures at the mileages you actually care about. Its limit is that US-only trucks barely exist in the UK fleet. No open dataset exists for comfort at all.

The cost numbers behind every point come from an eleven-tab workbook: download vehicle-turnover-planner.xlsx (117 KB, Excel). Its purchase prices are placeholders — replace them with real local listings before trusting any figure here.
